Washington State Department of Health

Washington radon (air)

No state license roster to republish. Updated / retrieved 2026-10-01 (US/Pacific).

If you are not sure you need a pro yet, see when a DIY radon test kit is enough or how to test a home for radon.

Washington DOH does not publish a public statewide list of radon measurement or mitigation businesses and does not offer a state radon license roster on its site. This page does not invent company names, phones, licenses, or addresses to fill that gap.

DOH’s “Hiring a Certified Radon Professional” section points homeowners to nationally certified providers instead: search through NRPP or NRSB Find a Professional (the same links DOH publishes). Filter by measurement vs. mitigation, then re-check the credential before hiring. For general contracting work, DOH also links Washington Labor & Industries — Hiring a Contractor.

What DOH says homeowners can do

  • Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas from rocks and soils. You cannot see, smell, or taste it — testing indoor air is the only way to know a building’s level.
  • EPA’s recommended action level remains 4.0 pCi/L (about 10 times typical outdoor air). DOH notes that other countries recommend action closer to 2.7 pCi/L; the goal is to reduce indoor radon as much as reasonably possible.
  • Test where people spend time (living areas and bedrooms), not unused broom closets or crawl spaces. Short-term kits (about 3–4 days with closed-house conditions), long-term tests, or continuous monitors can all measure radon; interpret results with the testing conditions in mind.
  • DOH has offered a limited supply of free radon test kits for Washington homes. Request through the DOH form: doh.wa.gov/form/radon-test-kit-access-form (DOH asks you to request a kit only if you will use it; allow time for delivery).
  • If a test shows elevated levels, DOH describes simple DIY steps (increase outdoor air exchange when appropriate, seal foundation cracks, maintain crawl-space vapor barriers and foundation vents) and then retest. If levels stay high, hire a certified radon professional via NRPP or NRSB.
  • Radon maps and past test data are available through the Washington Tracking Network (WTN) for context only — they are not a substitute for testing your own home.
  • Questions: Washington State Indoor Radon Program — doh.radon@doh.wa.gov or 360-236-3200.
